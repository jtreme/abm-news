Brandon Estevez: Where the avocado supply chain breaks

Presenting "Ripe for Disaster" at the Fall Spooky Research Slam, Thursday, Oct. 29, 4:30–6 p.m., Fishbowl Forum, Hill Library.

Brandon is an ABM student doing self-directed research through the Office of Undergraduate Research, with Dr. Kelsey Vourazeris as his faculty mentor. His first project asks what cheap avocados are hiding. We asked him six questions.

Answers lightly edited for length.
Question 1

What research are you presenting?

The title is "Ripe for Disaster: Where the Avocado Supply Chain Breaks." The purpose is to show how fragile the avocado supply chain is. Right now I'm working on showing how low avocado prices for U.S. consumers ignore a host of negative externalities: deforestation, cartel violence, foreign dependence, threats to USDA inspectors, and more. The end goal is a model that shows how those variables work together to affect avocado prices. If there's more cartel violence in a region one month, how many dollars do avocado prices go up for U.S. consumers?

Question 2

What first drew you to this question?

This project is the first in a larger series on supply chain fragility that I'd like to carry through my undergraduate years. I want to look at conflict minerals (using iPhones), labor dependency (using T-shirts), and environmental and violent externalities for local communities (using avocados). I've always been curious about how the complex systems around us work and how they're made. I want to learn why the world works the way it does and how it can be improved.

Each of those examples tells a larger social and geopolitical story that people just aren't exposed to enough. I want people to see the positive and negative effects globalization has had on their everyday lives, rather than the sweeping generalization that you either love it or demonize it. I chose avocados first because I'm taking ARE 311, which covers commodity and agricultural markets, and because Dr. V, who agreed to mentor the project, has expertise in this area and has done research on tomatoes in Mexico.

"If you eat an avocado here in North Carolina, it was likely grown by farmers 2,000 miles away you'll never meet, moved on trucks and trains you'll never see up close, and brought through dozens of buildings before it hit the grocery store."
Brandon Estevez, ABM
Question 3

What is the one finding you most want people to take away?

The products you consume every day are the result of systems, supply chains, and people you'll likely never see but that very much exist. Dozens of people, multimillion-dollar machines, and firms worked along the way to make sure the avocado you bought was pest-free, safe to eat, and fresh. When people realize how complex, expensive, and interconnected these systems are, they look at their purchases differently.

Question 4

Is there a common misconception your research pushes against?

A common assumption is that if avocados are always in stock and cheap, the supply chain behind them must be strong. My research suggests the opposite can be true. Stable prices can hide how dependent the system is on a few regions, a few inspections, and conditions staying calm. The goal of the model is to measure how much a disruption would actually cost, so we're not just guessing how fragile it is.

Question 5

Does this research show up in your classes?

It isn't part of any class I'm taking right now, and I like that. The project is self-directed and can evolve as I find different angles or learn more. The Office of Undergraduate Research structures its program so that students like me still have guidelines and resources to succeed even when the work isn't tied to a class, internship, or study abroad. They have resources for students in those situations too.

Question 6

If a student wanted to get involved in research like this, what's the first step?

Figure out a question you're interested in enough to learn about and determined enough to answer. Then find a connection, angle, or niche in the field that hasn't been explored and that you're set up to shed light on. At NC State, the Office of Undergraduate Research has great articles on how to get a project started, and how to get it paid for through Federal Work Study if you qualify. Their team is at D. H. Hill, and they've been more than happy to answer my questions or point me to funding, the Libraries, or chances to present.
